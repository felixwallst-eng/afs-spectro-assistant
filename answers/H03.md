## Recommended methods

- **Mid‑IR (MIR) – ATR probe.** Worth testing if the broth is a liquid or soft paste and an IR‑transparent fiber can be installed; MIR offers molecular absorption that can correlate with glucose or biomass when a suitable calibration is defined [S01 Table 1 (p.6)].
- **Raman – Raman probe.** Worth testing if the sample can be accessed with a probe (liquid, powder or solid) and the optical path is not dominated by scattering; Raman can provide compositional information without a fluorescent tag [S01 Table 1 (p.6)].
- **Near‑IR (NIR) – Transmission / transflection probe.** Worth testing if the broth is sufficiently transparent for transmission or can be measured in transflection mode; NIR can quantify glucose and biomass through multivariate models when the optical variability is manageable [S01 Table 1 (p.6)].

## Measurement concept

A fiber‑optic probe (ATR, Raman or transmission) would be positioned at the fermentation line, coupled to the existing spectrometer via a suitable fiber link; the probe geometry (contact, immersion or window) must match the sample state to ensure adequate optical coupling [S01 Table 1 (p.6)].

## Spectral range

MIR ATR: 4000–400 cm⁻¹ (2.5–25 µm); Raman excitation typically 785 nm with detection 400–1100 nm; NIR transmission: 780–2500 nm. The exact usable range depends on the fiber type and any spectral filters required – still to be confirmed.

## Risks, limitations and uncertainty

- Fluorescence alone is not selective; without a defined fluorophore it cannot directly report glucose or biomass (E3).
- If the sample is highly turbid or contains bubbles, transmission‑based methods may fail.
- Incorrect fiber choice could block the required spectral region.
- Calibration models may be invalid if optical properties change during the run.
