## Measurement concept

An immersible fiber‑optic probe (ATR, Raman or NIR) placed directly in the liquid stream, with the fiber link running to the spectrometer; the probe tip contacts the process fluid to ensure optical coupling [S01 p.5; S01 p.10; S04 p.1]. If a laboratory test is required, a cuvette or ATR plate can be used with the same fiber link.

## Spectral range

Mid‑IR 4000–400 cm⁻¹ (2.5–25 µm) for ATR or cuvette, Raman detection in the visible/short‑wave NIR (≈400–1100 nm) with typical 785 nm excitation, and NIR 780–2500 nm for transmission or diffuse reflection. The exact usable range must be confirmed against the available fiber type and length.

## Risks, limitations and uncertainty

- If the measurement interface must be non‑contact, all probe‑based methods drop out.
- High turbidity or bubbles could invalidate transmission‑based approaches.
- If only UV/Vis fibers are available, MIR methods cannot be used.
- Without a defined target species, methods such as fluorescence may be unsuitable.

## First feasibility study

1. 1. Obtain a small sample of the process liquid and measure its baseline optical properties (turbidity, absorption) with a benchtop spectrometer.
2. 2. Perform a laboratory feasibility test using a MIR ATR crystal or a cuvette to record spectra and identify any characteristic bands of potential contaminants.
3. 3. Run parallel Raman and NIR measurements on the same sample to compare signal strength and interference from scattering.
4. 4. Validate the laboratory spectra against an independent reference method (e.g., GC‑MS or HPLC) for known spiked contaminants.
5. 5. Assess fiber transmission losses for the available fiber type over the required length to confirm that the chosen spectral range can be delivered to an in‑line probe.

## Decision basis

A go/no‑go decision will be based on (i) confirmation that the target species produces a detectable spectral feature in the chosen range, (ii) demonstration that the probe can be installed in‑line without excessive fouling, and (iii) meeting the client‑defined detection limit and response time for the process control. Acceptance criteria must be defined by the client once the target variable and required sensitivity are known.
