## Measurement concept

A sealed ATR probe (e.g., ZrO₂ or diamond tip) inserted directly into the petroleum line, with the probe head in tight contact to the fluid [S04 p.4]; fiber link runs from probe to remote MIR analyzer. The current silica cable cannot transmit MIR wavelengths, so a MIR‑transparent fiber (chalcogenide, polycrystalline or hollow‑waveguide) would be required for the final system [S02 p.6].

## Spectral range

MIR ATR requires 2.5–25 µm; the existing silica cable only covers 0.35–2.4 µm, so the MIR range must be verified with a suitable fiber before final design [S02 p.6]. NIR feasibility can use 0.78–2.5 µm with the current cable.

## Risks, limitations and uncertainty

- Silica fiber will not transmit the required MIR wavelengths, making the proposed concept infeasible unless replaced [S02 p.6].
- If the petroleum is opaque or highly turbid, transmission‑based methods (NIR, Raman transmission) will fail [S01 p.16].
- Uncertain sample state may render ATR contact impossible or degrade signal quality.

## First feasibility study

1. 1. Obtain a representative sample of the petroleum at the intended probe location and characterise its physical state and optical clarity.
2. 2. Perform a laboratory Raman measurement on the sample using a standard Raman probe to assess water signal strength and interference.
3. 3. Test NIR transmission through a short length of the existing silica fiber with the sample in a cuvette to confirm usable signal levels.
4. 4. If Raman or NIR show promise, acquire a short segment of MIR‑transparent fiber (e.g., chalcogenide) and repeat a bench‑top ATR measurement with a compatible MIR source to verify water detection.
5. 5. Compare measured water concentrations against the required detection limit provided by the customer.
